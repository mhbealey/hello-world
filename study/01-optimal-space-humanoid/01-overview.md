# Section 01 — Optimal Space Humanoid: Overview

This section establishes the heritage baseline for the optimal space humanoid design. It documents what has been built, flown, or operationally demonstrated, identifies where existing designs fall short for space applications, and frames the questions that subsequent sections must answer.

### Heritage Gaps

The following capabilities are required for the space humanoid mission profile but are absent or undemonstrated in the heritage base reviewed above:

- **Microgravity mobility mode.** No heritage robot has a validated locomotion controller for handrail-based movement in zero-g or low-g environments. FEDOR's failure on ISS is the only data point, and it is a negative one. Surface bipedal locomotion and microgravity mobility are distinct unsolved problems that must both be addressed.
- **Vacuum and radiation hardening.** No commercial robot (Atlas, Figure 02, Optimus, Apollo, Digit, Unitree) has been qualified for vacuum operation, wide thermal cycling (−180 °C to +130 °C lunar surface), or the total ionizing dose (TID) environment of the lunar far side. R2 and FEDOR were qualified only for the pressurized ISS cabin environment, not open-space or surface conditions.
- **On-orbit / surface self-serviceability.** R2's grounding failure established that serviceability by crewmates must be a design requirement. No current commercial humanoid is designed with crew-accessible modular fault isolation in mind. All commercial platforms are designed for depot-level servicing on Earth.
- **Long-duration autonomous operation under communication latency.** No heritage robot has demonstrated sustained autonomous task execution under representative deep-space latency (>1.3 s one-way for lunar far side relay; up to 20 min for Mars). Current commercial autonomy is tuned for near-real-time or cloud-connected operation.
- **Dust tolerance and contamination control.** Lunar regolith (particle size 0.1–100 µm, electrostatically charged, abrasive) will penetrate seals and degrade actuators. No heritage robot has operated in a high-dust, fine-abrasive environment; R2 and FEDOR operated in filtered ISS air. This is an entirely open heritage gap.
- **Power budget compatibility with off-grid surface operations.** Robotic power budgets on the heritage robots range from ~300 W (estimated, Optimus) to ~1,800 W (estimated, Valkyrie). A lunar far side base power plant will have hard per-asset power allocations. No heritage robot has been operated within the constraints of a fixed renewable energy budget shared with life support.
- **Human-robot dexterous tool compatibility in EVA suit.** R2's hands were designed to use ISS crew tools without modification — the deepest heritage for EVA tool compatibility. No commercial robot replicates this design requirement. The EVA-gloved human hand is the relevant grasp-geometry target, and no current commercial platform has been tested against it.

## Open Questions (to be resolved in subsequent sections)

These questions are raised directly by the heritage review and must be resolved before the form-factor tradespace analysis (Section 01-02) can take positions:

1. **What is the binding mass constraint for the space humanoid?** The survey spans 35 kg (Unitree G1) to 150 kg (Robonaut 2 full config). Valkyrie's 129 kg was identified by NASA designers as a problem. The lander manifest calculus (Section 04-02) will set a hard ceiling; the form-factor tradespace must design to it. Preliminary working assumption: ≤100 kg in surface configuration, with margin. This assumption must be reconciled with the destinations-trajectories agent.

2. **What DOF count is actually required for the target task set?** The heritage survey shows no correlation between DOF count and deployment success. Digit (30 DOF) is the only production-deployed robot; Robonaut 2 (42 DOF) was the only ISS-deployed US robot and was disabled by an electrical fault unrelated to kinematics. The form-factor tradespace must define a minimum-sufficient DOF architecture against the specific task list from the ConOps section (03-02).

3. **Is series elastic actuation (Valkyrie heritage) or quasi-direct-drive (Unitree heritage) the right architecture for the primary joints?** SEA provides force control and impact tolerance; QDD provides backdrivability and simplicity. Both have terrestrial research heritage but neither has been operated in vacuum or under radiation. The answer has cascading implications for power draw, mass, and thermal management.

4. **What autonomy level is achievable and required under lunar far side latency?** Current commercial autonomy (Figure 02, Optimus) is tuned for near-zero latency AI inference with cloud connectivity. Lunar far side relay latency (Section 02-02 will quantify) makes cloud-dependent autonomy architecturally infeasible. The latency tradespace must specify an autonomy level achievable with fully on-board compute — this drives the sensing and computing subsystem mass and power allocations.

5. **Can a single form factor serve both surface EVA-support roles and microgravity transit roles, or does the mission require two distinct configurations?** FEDOR's failed microgravity adaptation and R2's stanchion-limited design suggest these may be incompatible in a single fixed architecture. The ConOps section must define whether the humanoid needs a zero-g mobility mode, and the form-factor tradespace must evaluate whether that can be achieved through software/controller adaptation alone or requires structural reconfiguration (e.g., foot-end-effectors for handrail grasp).
