## Attacks and Assessments

### DA-001 — BLOCKER — The form factor attack

**Attack:** The study's central commitment — full bipedal humanoid at 75 kg — is justified primarily on "program economics": the cost of maintaining dual tool and infrastructure standards over 20 years exceeds the bipedal form's mass and locomotion penalties. This is a claim about cumulative program costs over two decades. It is asserted rather than quantified. A Lunokhod-instinct critic would construct the following counter: a task-specialized architecture — two or three purpose-built variants (a logistics unit optimized for indoor flat-surface operation, an EVA-support unit with EVA-compatible hands on a quadrupedal base, a maintenance unit with two high-DOF arms on a fixed rail) — would collectively deliver lower total system mass, lower power draw per task, higher per-task reliability, and a less demanding autonomy TRL curve than a single general-purpose biped. Each variant could be designed to fit exactly the geometry it must operate in, rather than approximating the geometry of a human. The 0.10-point gap between the bipedal score (3.65) and the centaur score (3.55) in the tradespace evaluation is within any reasonable scoring uncertainty, meaning the "win" for the bipedal form rests entirely on the C1 criterion weight of 0.25 — a weight the authors themselves assigned and which assumes the base is human-built before robots arrive. If the robot-first base design is on the table at all (and the study raises it in the Section 5 dissent), the C1 weighting is circular: the base is designed for humans because humans will be there, but robots arrive first, so the base could have been designed for the robots that arrive first.

**Section's current response:** The section engages the Lunokhod counterargument directly and substantively (Section 4, third through fifth paragraphs). It argues that purpose-built beats generalist for single-destination fixed-duration missions but fails when robots must share tools, hatches, and emergency procedures with human crew — that the cost of duplicating all human-geometry infrastructure for a different robot form exceeds the cost of building a robot that fits existing human geometry. Section 5 (Dissenting Position) states the conditions under which a centaur would be the correct answer and explicitly characterizes the program schedule risk that would reverse the decision (robot deployment preceding base infrastructure by five or more years). The tradespace scoring discrepancy is acknowledged: "The bipedal form wins primarily on C1 (human tool compatibility, highest weight) and C7 (training). It loses on mass and scores only average on surface locomotion — neither of which should be papered over."

**Assessment:** The response is substantive but the core quantitative gap is real and is not closed. The "program economics" argument is stated as a conclusion — "the cost of maintaining parallel tool and infrastructure standards over a permanent base lifetime outweighs the locomotion and mass penalties of the bipedal form" — without a supporting calculation. No attempt is made to estimate: how many unique tool types exist in the base, what fraction require human-geometry access, what fraction a specialized robot could use with minor modification, or what the actual recurring cost differential is between a one-robot-type program and a two-robot-type program over 20 years. This is not a detail — it is the thesis. A reviewer at a program review board would ask for this number immediately. Without it, the entire form factor argument rests on an unquantified economic assertion whose direction may be correct but whose magnitude is unknown. The study cannot defend its foundational choice against a well-resourced alternative-architecture proposal without this analysis. Additionally, the study commits to bipedal form before robots arrive and before humans arrive; the Section 5 dissent acknowledges this could reverse the decision but does not explore the actual deployment sequence in sufficient depth to close it. The form factor decision precedes the ConOps, which means the economics argument is being made before the task list is fully defined — a logical circularity.

**Required action:** Humanoid-systems-architect must add a parametric economic comparison. Minimum acceptable: (a) a rough task taxonomy for the base, broken down by tool-geometry requirement; (b) a parametric estimate of the per-tool and per-infrastructure-interface cost delta between single-standard and dual-standard over a 20-year program; (c) a sensitivity analysis showing at what point the dual-standard cost exceeds the bipedal mass penalty. This does not need to be precise — it needs to show that the economics argument is directionally correct and that the crossover point is in a reasonable range. If this analysis cannot be done until the ConOps section defines the task list, the form factor section must explicitly mark the economic conclusion as provisional pending that input, with a go/no-go gate.

### DA-002 — BLOCKER — The autonomy TRL attack

**Attack:** The §A1 autonomy assumption — TRL 6 in space-relevant environments by 2029, TRL 7+ by 2035 — is the load-bearing beam under the entire study architecture. Without it, the humanoid-forward model collapses to a permanently high-crew-time-overhead teleoperation system, which defeats the economic thesis. The study's honest summary of the autonomy stack (Section 01-04, Section 3) places the reactive layer at space TRL 3–4 and the deliberative layer at space TRL 2–3 as of 2026. To reach TRL 6 in space-relevant environments by 2029 — three years from now — the reactive layer needs to advance two TRL levels and the deliberative layer needs to advance three TRL levels, both in a lunar-analog environment with representative dust, gravity, and radiation, using hardware that does not yet exist. The TRL 6 milestone for the reactive layer requires hardware testing in a lunar gravity offload facility with JSC-1A simulant (Section 01-04 states this explicitly). No such test has been run. No specific test program is identified. No organization is named to run it. No cost estimate exists. The deliberative layer requires a lunar-analog task demonstration dataset that does not exist and hardware-in-the-loop validation on a first-article space humanoid that does not exist. Three years is not enough time to design a space humanoid first article, build it, test it in a lunar-analog environment, and close two TRL levels for the deliberative layer. The §A1 description says explicitly "this is a study assumption, not a forecast" — but that sentence does not make it a valid program commitment. It makes it a circular assumption: the study works if the autonomy curve holds; the autonomy curve is asserted without a specific demonstration program behind it; therefore the study works if its own ungrounded assumption holds. This is the one weakness that could invalidate the entire study.

**Section's current response:** The §A1 entry acknowledges that the curve is "a study assumption, not a forecast" and says it is treated as "a program commitment with explicit go/no-go gates at each TRL milestone." Section 01-04 is honest about the current space TRL values and explicitly states what is required to reach TRL 6 (hardware testing, state estimator validation, fall-and-recovery in low-gravity). The section correctly notes that go/no-go gates mean the timeline slips if the gate is missed. The study does not hide the gap; it acknowledges it directly.

**Assessment:** Acknowledgment is not closure. The problem is not that the study fails to flag this risk — it does, repeatedly and honestly. The problem is that the §A1 curve has no specific backing program that would make TRL 6 by 2029 credible. Section 01-04 says items (1) and (2) of the TRL 6 requirements "are achievable in existing NASA facilities by 2029" — this is an unsupported claim. Which facility? Funded under which program? With what hardware? By whom? Item (3) — controlled fall and recovery in low-gravity — "requires either a parabolic flight campaign or a dedicated low-gravity analog facility." Neither has been identified. The §A1 assumption admits it is not a forecast, but the study is supposed to be a "program backbone" (CLAUDE.md: "the core bones of a program, not an academic paper"). A program backbone without an identified TRL 5 demonstration as the first milestone is a schedule with a critical path item that has no resources behind it. The TRL 7 by 2035 gate depends on first-article hardware availability by 2031–2032; no first-article program is identified. This is the study's deepest vulnerability: the technology program that would make the thesis credible is not described in sufficient detail to be actionable. The 2029 TRL 6 claim is particularly exposed — three years from now, with current space TRL at 2–4 across all layers, it would require a funded, executing program to start immediately. No such program is named.

**Required action:** Technology-roadmap-trl agent must add a TRL 5 demonstration program as the first concrete milestone: a specific test campaign (facility, platform, test objectives, success criteria, lead organization, rough cost bracket, and schedule) that would demonstrate reactive layer TRL 5 by 2027 and TRL 6 by 2029. This is the minimum that makes the §A1 assumption defensible as a program commitment rather than a study assumption. If this program cannot be described (because it has not been planned), the §A1 curve must be treated as aspirational with the first gate explicitly unfunded, and the study's deployment schedule must explicitly show the consequence of a 2-year gate slip. The missing TRL 5 demo identification is a blocker because without it the study cannot function as program input — which is its stated purpose.

### DA-003 — MAJOR — The mass attack

**Attack:** The 75 kg design-to target is established by comparison to commercial, non-space-qualified bipedal robots. Optimus Gen 2 at 57 kg and Atlas Electric at 89 kg are the bracket. Neither has been qualified for vacuum, thermal cycling to −180°C, radiation at 20–30 krad(Si)/yr, or lunar dust. The study's own analysis identifies the mass additions required for space qualification: labyrinth + seal housing mass across 38 joints (~4 kg), radiation shielding for compute (~1.8 kg including spot shields), torso structural radiation shielding (~1 kg), MLI blanket and heater hardware (~0.8 kg + heater elements), CFRP structural requirement (conditional on material survivability), FFKM-sealed connectors, boot covers, N₂ canister. The study acknowledges these additions and closes the budget at 58.2 kg of allocated subsystems against the 75 kg target, leaving 16.8 kg of system growth allowance. The adversarial question is: does a 16.8 kg growth allowance (22% of total design-to) adequately capture the cost of going from a commercial terrestrial robot to a space-qualified one? The study itself says the appropriate concept-phase margin for a system with this many sub-TRL-5 items is 35–40%, not 30% (Section 01-06, Section 3). If that recommendation were followed, the design-to target would need to be ~67–68 kg (to preserve 35% margin against the 97.5 kg NTE), and the commercial heritage bracket no longer closes — Atlas Electric at 89 kg is above the 97.5 kg NTE. The mass argument for bipedal viability rests on the assumption that the 75 kg design-to number survives detailed design — an assumption the study itself qualifies as medium-high risk.

**Section's current response:** The mass budget section (01-06) is explicit about the stress points: the power system is the largest single mass item (16.9 kg, sensitive to energy density assumptions), and the budget recommends treating 30% as minimum margin rather than available headroom, explicitly noting that 35–40% would be more appropriate for this TRL profile. Section 01-02 passed the heritage bracket (57–89 kg commercial to 129 kg Valkyrie) to downstream sections, and the form factor tradespace scored the bipedal form a "4" on mass (not a "5"). §A2 correctly flags mass as "high" risk.

**Assessment:** The section partially addresses this attack. The honest framing of the margin situation is a genuine strength — the study does not claim the budget is comfortable. However, there is a structural flaw in the comparison: the study uses commercial, non-space-qualified robots as the lower end of the heritage bracket while simultaneously acknowledging that space qualification adds mass. This creates a false baseline: 57 kg (Optimus) is not the low end of what a space humanoid could be — it is the low end of what a terrestrial commercial humanoid has achieved. The appropriate comparison for the 75 kg claim would be: what does a space-qualified Optimus-class robot actually weigh? The study cannot answer this because no such robot has been built. The 16.8 kg growth allowance is the study's buffer for this uncertainty, but the study recommends (correctly) that it should be larger. The net result is that the mass target is plausible but not demonstrated, and the section's own risk assessment acknowledges the SEA fallback alone could add 10–20 kg and potentially breach the NTE. This is a known path to failure that has not been fully accounted for in the form factor selection.

**Required action:** Humanoid-systems-architect should add a paragraph explicitly quantifying what space-qualification adds to a representative commercial platform mass, using any available heritage data (such as ISS equipment space-qualification mass penalties, FEDOR's 106 kg vs. commercial bipeds of similar DOF). The goal is to show that 75 kg design-to is a credible starting point for a space-qualified platform, not just that 75 kg commercial bipeds exist. Additionally, the section should address what happens to the bipedal form factor selection if the SEA fallback is triggered at the 2029 gate: at 85–95 kg design-to (after adding 10–20 kg for SEA), does the form factor selection change?

### DA-004 — MAJOR — The power attack

**Attack:** The 500 W steady-state / 800 W peak power budget is derived by scaling Valkyrie's estimated 1,800 W by mass ratio (75/129) and applying a "15% efficiency improvement credit for next-generation electric actuators." The Atlas Electric is cited as evidence that HD-Electric actuation has 85–90% electrical-to-mechanical efficiency. However, this conflates actuator efficiency with system power draw. A high-efficiency actuator draws less power per unit of mechanical output — but the required mechanical output for bipedal locomotion is determined by the physics of the gait, not by actuator efficiency. Boston Dynamics Atlas Electric (89 kg) has not published its operational power draw. The Valkyrie comparison is with SEA actuators, which the study rejects on mass grounds — so using Valkyrie's power draw as the scaling anchor means scaling from the wrong architecture. More specifically: the locomotion actuation budget (260 W design-to for hips, knees, ankles) is derived by applying a "0.55 factor for normal vs. vigorous gait" on top of the mass-ratio scaling, which the section itself acknowledges as "a parametric assumption with no direct heritage validation for this platform." The power budget's locomotion actuation line — the single largest line item at 260 W — is therefore the product of two unvalidated scaling factors applied to a Valkyrie-heritage anchor that used a different actuation architecture.

**Section's current response:** Section 01-06 explicitly acknowledges the uncertainty: "The study uses 260 W based on an additional 0.55 factor for normal vs. vigorous gait — a parametric assumption with no direct heritage validation for this platform. This is acknowledged as a budget stress point." §A3 in the margins register states the derivation fully, including the scaling basis and the 15% efficiency credit, and acknowledges the 500 W steady-state figure is "aggressive and is flagged as requiring validation by the actuation subsystem design." The section also flags that a task-level simulation is needed to validate the peak draw assumption for the 38-joint simultaneous-load case.

**Assessment:** The section is correctly self-aware about the power budget's weak basis. The problem is not concealment — it is that the acknowledged uncertainty is large. The locomotion actuation line (260 W) is the dominant item in the full-locomotion mode (55% of the 474 W pre-margin design-to), and it rests on two unjustified parametric adjustments. The study correctly calls this a stress point but does not give the reader a sense of what the risk envelope looks like. If the actual locomotion power draw for a 75 kg HD-Electric biped on lunar regolith is 400 W (which is within the heritage uncertainty), the total design-to becomes ~614 W, the margin-included total becomes ~798 W, and the budget barely closes against the 800 W cap — leaving no actual margin for the manipulation load that will be running simultaneously. The study needs to show the failure mode and the consequence, not just flag the stress point.

**Required action:** Add a sensitivity table to Section 01-06 or §A3: at 1.5× the design-to locomotion power (390 W), what happens to the full-locomotion-plus-manipulation budget? Does it close? If not, what subsystem gets derated? This is the kind of bounding analysis that makes a concept paper credible. Also: the study should identify one specific test that would validate the locomotion power draw assumption at the correct scale, similar to how it identifies specific test programs for the dust seal and flexspline TRL gaps.

### DA-005 — MAJOR — The lunar night attack

**Attack:** The thermal hibernation power budget is the only place in the entire budget that explicitly does not close — the upper bound (304 W with margin) exceeds the stated goal (150 W) by 2×. The section acknowledges this and correctly identifies the cause: TRL 2 parametric estimate with a 3× uncertainty span (70–200 W range), driven by the bipedal form's large surface-area-to-volume ratio. The study then states that the detailed thermal model "must reach TRL 5 by 2031" — five years from now. The adversarial point is stronger than the section acknowledges: the form factor tradespace scored the bipedal form against criterion C5 (mass, 0.10 weight) but did not include thermal management as a separate criterion or as a penalty within an existing criterion. The directive from Section 01-02 to Section 01-05 explicitly flagged that "the bipedal form has large surface-area-to-volume ratio compared to a compact centaur or fixed platform" and that "thermal analysis must account for radiative and conductive heat paths through all four limbs" — but this analysis was not completed before the form factor position was locked. The tradespace therefore selected the bipedal form without knowing whether its thermal behavior was compatible with the base power architecture. If the actual thermal draw resolves to 200 W per humanoid (the upper bound), three hibernating humanoids need 600 W of FSP capacity during every 14-day lunar night — continuously. A 10 kWe FSP plant running a base with life support, science instruments, habitat heating, and other loads must reserve 6% of its capacity indefinitely for robot thermal maintenance. This is not a trivial requirement and it was not traded against the alternative form factors.

**Section's current response:** Section 01-02 explicitly flags the thermal liability of the bipedal form as a direction to Section 01-05: "Thermal analysis must account for radiative and conductive heat paths through all four limbs." Section 01-05 develops this into the 70–200 W range and flags it as a TRL 2 uncertainty. Section 01-06 closes only at the lower bound and explicitly states the FSP consequence at the upper bound (900 W with three humanoids). The study does not hide this. §A10 flags it with medium risk.

**Assessment:** The section partially responds but the form factor decision is immune to the thermal finding because the thermal finding came after the form factor decision. The section cannot retroactively change the form factor score — it can only flag the thermal uncertainty as a downstream risk. The missing analysis is: what would the centaur's thermal hibernation power draw be? If the centaur's compact lower body reduces the thermal reservation by 50% (reasonable given the surface-area-to-volume difference), the bipedal form's effective FSP reservation advantage over a centaur evaporates — one of the centaur's disadvantages (C5 mass score of 2, vs. bipedal's 4) was based on total system mass, not on FSP lifetime power reservation. The bipedal form may be more expensive over the program lifetime in FSP consumption even if it is cheaper on lander manifest. This cross-trade has not been done.

**Required action:** Add a direct thermal comparison between bipedal and centaur form factors for the hibernation case. Even a parametric estimate (centaur torso identical to bipedal; centaur base tracked/wheeled and passively insulated; bipedal four-limb surface area approximately 2× centaur base surface area at equivalent total mass) would strengthen or weaken the form factor argument. If the centaur has substantially lower thermal reservation requirements, this must either be reflected in the form factor scoring (add a thermal criterion with non-zero weight) or be defended explicitly as an acceptable trade against C1 (tool compatibility). The current state — acknowledging the thermal liability without propagating it back to the form factor selection — leaves the selection incompletely defended.

### DA-006 — MAJOR — The servicing attack

**Attack:** The study makes crew serviceability a first-class criterion (C4, weight 0.15) and cites R2's disabling failure as the canonical negative lesson: "A robot that cannot be serviced by a crewmate in EVA gloves is a single-fault total loss at the lunar far side." It then scores the bipedal form a "3" on C4, noting that serviceability "is possible in principle; requires modular design discipline that no heritage system demonstrates." The critical question the sections do not address is whether the humanoid is itself the subject of serviceability by a second humanoid. On a permanent base with multiple humanoid units, the most natural serviceability model is peer serviceability: when unit A fails, unit B (under remote supervision) performs the repair. This is architecturally significant because it means the humanoid must be able to service a second humanoid — and the study's position that all ORU access is designed for EVA-gloved human hands means the humanoid's own hands must also be able to perform these operations. The section establishes the 50 mm clear access and single-fastener removal requirement (Section 01-02, constraints to actuation section) but never explicitly closes the loop: can the humanoid's own hands perform the ORU replacement sequences it would need to perform on a peer unit? This is both a dexterity requirement (10–12 DOF hands must be able to torque ORU fasteners in the size range defined by the ORU access requirement) and a reach requirement (can the humanoid reach all of its own ORU access panels on a peer unit that is in a maintenance posture?). If the answer is yes, this is a major capability enhancement that should be explicit in the ConOps. If the answer is no, the serviceability model requires human EVA for every robotic ORU replacement — which compounds with the human-in-the-loop analysis in ways that Section 02 will need to address. Neither answer appears in the current sections.

**Section's current response:** Section 01-02 establishes the ORU serviceability requirement ("All external connectors and joint access panels must be sized for EVA-gloved hands (minimum 50 mm clear access, single-fastener removal per ORU)"). Section 01-05 notes that the ORU replacement strategy requires "the base workshop to have the capability to replace electronics boards, which is a conops and logistics requirement, not merely a design requirement." No section discusses peer humanoid serviceability or quantifies what fraction of fault responses require human EVA versus can be executed by a second humanoid unit.

**Assessment:** The serviceability discussion stops at the mechanical design requirement level and never reaches the operational consequence level. This is a gap with significant program implications. In a permanent base scenario, if every Tier 2 compute board replacement (3-year cycle, per §A11) requires a human EVA, the base must dedicate crew EVA hours to robot maintenance. Over a 7-year design life with three humanoids, this is up to 6 Tier 2 replacements per unit × 3 units × (EVA time for board replacement, estimated several hours) = potentially 50–100 crew EVA hours for routine compute maintenance alone. This number must appear somewhere in the ConOps. The current sections do not provide it.

**Required action:** Humanoid-systems-architect or conops-integrator must add a serviceability scenario analysis: for the top three ORU types (Tier 2 compute board, foot/ankle boot cover assembly, joint seal assembly), specify (a) whether the operation is human EVA-required or humanoid-robot-executable, (b) estimated crew time per operation, and (c) frequency based on the design-life assumptions. This feeds directly into the ConOps human-in-the-loop analysis and the cost-program ORU logistics model.

### DA-007 — MINOR — The TRL path attack

**Attack:** Even accepting the §A1 autonomy curve as a program commitment, the path from current space TRL to TRL 8 by 2038–2040 requires a coherent sequence of demonstration programs. The study implies this sequence but does not specify any of the intermediate milestones as executable programs. TRL 5 demonstration by approximately 2027–2028 is the first milestone that must be backed by a specific test campaign, and none is identified. Without at least the first milestone specified — who, what, where, how much — the roadmap is a timeline chart with empty cells, not a program.

**Section's current response:** Section 01-04 states that TRL 6 reactive layer requirements are "achievable in existing NASA facilities by 2029" and notes a parabolic flight campaign or low-gravity analog facility is needed for item (3). The §A1 assumption states that "if the 2029 gate is missed, the deployment timeline slips proportionally." The section correctly identifies what must happen; it does not identify the specific program that will make it happen.

**Assessment:** This is a legitimate gap that the technology-roadmap-trl agent is the correct owner of, not the sensing-autonomy or actuation sections. The sensing-autonomy section has correctly characterized the technical requirements and handed them off. The gap is not in the sections reviewed here; it is in the technology roadmap section (04-01, not yet written). The weakness is partially attributable to incomplete adjacent sections. However, the sections here should at minimum name a candidate TRL 5 demonstration: e.g., "a 2027 testing campaign using a commercial bipedal humanoid modified with JSC-1A simulant exposure in a reduced-gravity test stand (NASA Langley drop tower or MSFC equivalent) would serve as the TRL 5 reactive-layer demonstration." The absence of even a candidate framework makes the 2029 gate appear unbacked.

**Required action:** Section 01-04 (autonomy stack) should add a one-paragraph "First gate definition" subsection within the autonomy/teleoperation boundary section, naming a specific candidate TRL 5 test architecture for the reactive layer (even at concept-paper level: test platform, facility type, test objectives, success criteria). This is a handoff sharpener for the technology-roadmap-trl agent. Without it, the roadmap agent has no anchor for the first milestone.

### DA-008 — MINOR — The "why humanoid first-principles" attack

**Attack:** Every argument for the humanoid form factor assumes the base infrastructure is human-scale. But the 2035 IOC for humanoid deployment precedes any credible permanent human presence on the lunar far side. During the 2035–2040 window, robots operate the base without resident crew. In this window, the C1 criterion (human tool compatibility, weight 0.25, the criterion the bipedal form wins on) has near-zero value — there are no humans in the base using human tools, sharing lockers, going through human-geometry hatches. The form factor that wins in the absence of humans is the one optimized for robotic efficiency — which is not the bipedal form. This is not a permanent argument against bipedal humanoids: once permanent human presence is established (2040+), the C1 criterion recovers full weight. The question is whether a two-phase architecture — robot-optimized phase 2035–2040, transition to human-compatible phase 2040+ — provides a better system economics picture than designing for human compatibility from the start at the cost of 12–14 years of suboptimal robotic efficiency. The study does not engage this two-phase possibility.

**Section's current response:** Section 5 (Dissenting Position) addresses the condition under which a robot-first base design would be correct: "a centaur becomes clearly superior is the one where the base infrastructure investment precedes the first humanoid deployment by five or more years — long enough to design the base for the robot rather than vice versa." The section treats this as a scheduling risk, not as a planned architecture. The study assumes a human-first design intent from the beginning, even for the 2035 IOC phase.

**Assessment:** The sections partially address this but treat the two-phase scenario as a scheduling risk rather than exploring it as an architecture option. The form factor scoring does not include a temporal dimension — the weight of C1 should arguably be lower for the 2035 IOC phase and higher for the 2040+ human-occupied phase, and the form factor that maximizes integrated performance over the full timeline may not be the same as the form factor that maximizes performance at the permanent human presence phase alone. This is a genuine architectural question the tradespace elides. The study's position is defensible but would be stronger if it explicitly addressed the temporal weighting of C1 and concluded that designing for 2040+ from the start is the correct program economics choice.

**Required action:** Section 01-02 should add a paragraph addressing the IOC temporal mismatch: acknowledge that for the 2035–2040 phase, C1 has reduced value because humans are not resident, quantify the approximate duration of this phase, and argue explicitly why designing for the 2040+ state from the start (accepting suboptimal robot efficiency in 2035–2040) is more cost-effective than a phased transition. If the answer involves avoiding a costly mid-program hardware redesign, say so explicitly and bound the cost of the alternative.

### DA-009 — NIT — Heritage comparison internal consistency

**Attack:** The study uses Atlas Electric (89 kg) as a heritage anchor for HD-Electric actuation efficiency (85–90%) and as the lower end of the mass bracket for bipedal robots. It simultaneously acknowledges that Atlas Electric's rated power draw is "unverified" and "unpublished." The power budget then scales from Valkyrie (not Atlas Electric) for the locomotion power estimate. This means the key efficiency data point (Atlas Electric's 85–90% efficiency) is cited for architectural justification but never used quantitatively in the budget, which derives from a different, less efficient heritage platform (Valkyrie SEA). The efficiency credit is being claimed without the corresponding calculation.

**Section's current response:** None — the budget uses the Valkyrie mass-ratio scaling and separately cites Atlas's efficiency figure without integrating them into a single derivation.

**Assessment:** Minor internal inconsistency that a reviewer would flag but that does not affect the directional conclusions. The budget acknowledges uncertainty at the stress point; the inconsistency in heritage use is a clarity issue, not a logic error.

**Required action:** Add a footnote or parenthetical in Section 01-06 (actuation power notes) showing the Atlas-Electric-based derivation as a check: if Atlas Electric at 89 kg and 85–90% efficiency has an estimated locomotion power draw of X W (based on inverse mass-ratio scaling from the 260 W design-to), does X appear reasonable given Atlas's operational context? This closes the internal consistency gap with one sentence.

## Overall Assessment

The study is substantively stronger than an early concept-phase document has any obligation to be. The sections are honest about their limitations, flag open TRL gaps with specificity, and avoid papering over uncertainty. The biggest structural vulnerability — DA-001 and DA-002 — are both cases where an unquantified assertion is carrying thesis weight. DA-001 (economics of dual tool standards) is the foundational case for the humanoid form; DA-002 (autonomy TRL 6 by 2029) is the foundational case for the teaming model. Both can be addressed without redesigning the architecture — they need quantitative scaffolding, not different conclusions. The four Major findings (DA-003 through DA-006) are genuine gaps that weaken specific arguments but do not threaten the study's thesis. The two Minor findings and one Nit are fixable with targeted additions to existing sections.

The study's conservative instincts — acknowledging that 35–40% margin would be more appropriate than 30%, recommending the 30% be treated as a floor not headroom, flagging the lunar night thermal budget as the only place that does not close — are a genuine program discipline strength. These acknowledgments will buy credibility with a technical review board. The missing economic and programmatic specificity (cost of dual standards, named TRL 5 demo program) is the gap that a program review board would exploit to delay or redirect the work. Closing DA-001 and DA-002 is the priority.
