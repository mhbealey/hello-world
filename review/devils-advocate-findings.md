# Devil's Advocate Review Findings — Stage 8
## Question (b): Human-in-the-Loop Value

*Sections reviewed: 02-01 (Overview), 02-02 (Latency Tradespace), 02-03 (Autonomy TRL and Task Allocation), 02-04 (Teaming Model)*

---

### DA-5 — Blocker — "Humans add nothing if autonomy is good enough"

**Attack:** The strongest version of this attack does not say autonomy is already adequate — it says the study's §A1 TRL curve is the only structural element preventing the HITL architecture from collapsing. If VLA foundation models reach TRL 8 by 2035 rather than 2038–2040, eighteen of the twenty far-side base tasks could plausibly migrate to autonomy-led. At that point, the six jointly-executed tasks shrink toward two or three, the 4-person crew requirement drops toward two, and the forward-deployment commitment becomes a very expensive preference rather than a necessity. The study defines a "human value floor" of seven categories — but a skeptical program reviewer will observe that these seven categories were defined after the architecture was committed to forward deployment. They correspond precisely to tasks that are hard to automate in 2026. By 2035, medical triage algorithms are credibly TRL 8 (FDA-approved diagnostic AI systems are already cleared for clinical use with physician oversight). Emergency response protocols at TRL 8 are feasible for pre-specified scenarios. Science priority decisions are increasingly assisted by AI systems. The attack, stated bluntly: the seven-category floor is a 2026 snapshot of "things robots cannot do yet" dressed up as a permanent program commitment. A program review board will notice that the floor was constructed after the crew-presence commitment was made, not before — and will ask whether the commitment can be maintained if TRL advances faster than §A1 predicts.

**Section's current response:** Section 02-03 engages the counter-case explicitly in Section 5 ("Counter-Case: Do High-Autonomy Humanoids Need Human Supervision?") with three arguments ordered by weight: (1) exploration is structurally OOD; (2) consequence asymmetry — the cost of an authorization gate (2–5 minutes) against the cost of an autonomous failure in a life-safety context is so large that the burden of proof lies with those who want to remove the gate; (3) Lunokhod operators solved problems that autonomous systems could not, so the lesson is to design the human-autonomy interface for speed, not eliminate humans. Section 02-01 states that the floor "does not shrink as TRL advances, because exploration novelty does not diminish with robot maturity." Section 02-03 Section 3 states: "What remains permanently human-required regardless of TRL: Life-safety decisions; any action that is irreversible outside the robot itself; first execution of any new task type; explicit crew override for any reason."

**Assessment:** The response is directionally correct but structurally exposed at two specific points. First, the study invokes the OOD argument as if it is a permanent structural feature of exploration, but the argument weakens as lunar base operations mature. In years 5–10 of base operation, the vast majority of encountered situations will not be novel — the base has been running for years, the terrain is mapped, the task library is large, and the robots have accumulated operational hours. Exploration novelty is highest at the beginning; the permanent-base context shifts over time toward operational maturity where OOD frequency declines. The study does not acknowledge this temporal dimension. Second, the consequence-asymmetry argument is valid for crew-contact and life-safety tasks, but it does not cover science priority decisions or medical triage. The study claims "science priority decisions — which sample to collect, which anomaly to investigate — are in the human value floor," but this is a policy decision, not a physics or consequence-asymmetry argument. By 2035, an AI geologist with access to hyperspectral imagery may make better science priority decisions than a fatigued crew member at hour six of a monitoring shift. The study states this is "scientific judgment that humans are not delegating to autonomous systems on this program regardless of model capability" — but this is program policy, not a principled floor, and a reviewer will read it as exactly that. The consequence-asymmetry argument covers categories 2, 3, 4, and 5 of the floor convincingly. It does not convincingly cover categories 1 (first execution authorization, which is a learning-period requirement that fades), 6 (science priority), or 7 (OOD-flag response, which depends entirely on how well-calibrated the robot's self-model becomes by 2035). The three categories that are least defensible as permanent floors are also the ones where VLA advancement has the most leverage.

**Required action:** Section 02-03 must distinguish between two types of human value floor categories: (A) permanently load-bearing regardless of TRL, grounded in physics or irreducible consequence asymmetry (categories 2, 3, 4, 5 — crew contact, pressurized interfaces, habitat breach, structural modification); and (B) load-bearing at 2035 IOC but subject to revision at subsequent program gates as TRL and operational maturity advance (categories 1, 6, 7 — first execution, science priority, OOD response). Type A categories must be stated as permanent with specific justification; Type B must be stated as 2035 program positions with gate criteria for revision. Currently all seven are stated with equal permanence, which overstates the floor's defensibility and makes it vulnerable to a single counterexample. Additionally, Section 02-01 must acknowledge that the OOD argument weakens as base operations mature and explain why forward deployment remains justified even in a late-mission operational maturity scenario.

### DA-6 — Major — "The 2.78 s relay latency makes the case for full autonomy, not for forward-deployed humans"

**Attack:** The strongest version of this attack is not that forward deployment is wrong — it is that the study has not eliminated a cheaper alternative. The physics argument for forward deployment is: 2.78 s RTLT from Earth is inadequate for real-time supervision of jointly-executed tasks. The study's response is: therefore humans must be physically at the lunar far side. But there is a third option the study does not seriously evaluate: redesign the jointly-executed tasks to not require real-time supervision. Specifically — if the six jointly-executed tasks at IOC were redesigned as fully autonomous tasks with pre-validated parameters and post-hoc human review, the real-time supervision requirement disappears. The study itself shows that twelve of twenty tasks are autonomy-led at IOC without requiring real-time human intervention. Why cannot the six jointly-executed tasks be driven to the same category through design? The attack is sharpest on the two most operationally-constrained cases: EVA tool handoff (T09) and solar panel installation (T05). T09 requires crew contact confirmation — but if the tool handoff is designed with a physical interlock that the crew member activates (a contact sensor on the receiving side, not the robot side), the "human authorization gate" becomes a dead-man switch held by the crew member, not a real-time supervision function. T05 requires human authorization at physical engagement gates — but if each gate is designed with a mechanical interlock that prevents engagement above a force threshold, the human authorization becomes a pre-mission parameter review rather than a real-time checkpoint. The study presents the jointly-executed category as requiring forward-deployed humans; it has not demonstrated that the tasks cannot be redesigned to eliminate that requirement. Forward deployment, at an estimated system cost that dwarfs robotic alternatives over a twenty-year lifetime, needs to clear a higher evidentiary bar than "these six tasks currently require real-time supervision."

**Section's current response:** Section 02-02 Section 3 explicitly excludes Earth-relay supervision as "not a viable primary operating mode for the task profile of the far-side base" and states this as the study's baseline. Section 02-04 develops the three-pillar forward-deployment case (latency, situational awareness, symbolic/operational continuity) and argues in Pillar 1 that the jointly-executed task checkpoint decisions require response within 5–30 seconds, making 5.56 s minimum Earth-relay exchange latency inadequate. Section 02-01 states that "Earth supervision cannot deliver sub-5-second response at 2.78 s RTLT." The closest the study comes to engaging the task-redesign path is Section 02-02's note that Earth supervision is "reserved for two specific use cases: scheduled, low-novelty pre-scripted operations, and anomaly response with deliberate delay acceptance."

**Assessment:** The study's response handles the latency-for-Earth-supervision branch of the attack well. It does not engage the task-redesign branch at all. This is the specific gap: the study moves directly from "2.78 s RTLT eliminates real-time Earth supervision" to "therefore forward-deployed humans" without engaging whether the tasks that require real-time supervision could be redesigned to not require it. This is not a trivial gap. The Lunokhod program operated entirely without forward deployment and produced forty-eight kilometers of traverse. The Mars rover program operates entirely without forward deployment and has produced decades of surface science. Both programs accommodated their task profiles to the supervision regime rather than insisting on forward deployment. The study's claim that the far-side base task profile "requires" real-time supervision is only defensible if it demonstrates that the specific tasks cannot be redesigned to eliminate that requirement. For T09 (EVA tool handoff), the crew contact requirement is a safety decision but physical interlocking designs exist for crew-contact safety applications in terrestrial robotics without requiring real-time human supervision. For T05 (panel installation), the human authorization gate for physical engagement is described as required, but no analysis is shown of whether force-limiting interlocks or pre-validated parameter envelopes could substitute. The medical emergency case (Section 02-01, 02-02) is the strongest concrete argument for forward deployment because no redesign eliminates the need to drive a humanoid in real time during an evolving medical crisis — this case should be elevated and made the primary justification, with the jointly-executed task category as secondary support rather than primary.

**Required action:** Section 02-03 must add a subsection of two to three paragraphs explicitly engaging the task-redesign alternative for each of the six jointly-executed tasks and explaining why redesign fails. For each task, the argument must show either: (a) the real-time human judgment function cannot be replaced by a mechanical, parametric, or pre-validated substitute without unacceptable safety degradation; or (b) redesigning the task to eliminate real-time supervision would reduce scientific or operational value below mission requirements. Tasks where redesign is plausible (T16 radio telescope calibration, T14 anomaly reporting) should be honestly assessed as candidates for migration to autonomy-led, which would tighten the forward-deployment argument by concentrating it on the genuinely irreplaceable cases. The medical emergency scenario should be elevated to the primary concrete justification in Section 02-01. The current structure buries the strongest argument in the middle of the section.

### DA-7 — Major — "The 1:2-3 supervisor ratio is optimistic; NIP-10 ran 5:1 and that's the only real data point"

**Attack:** The supervisor ratio (1:2–3 humanoids per active supervisor at IOC, improving to 1:4–5 by 2040) is derived through a five-step chain, with each step adding an assumption. Step 1 is the NIP-10 empirical data point (5:1, no autonomy, 2.5 s RTLT). Steps 2 through 5 are model-derived: the §A1 TRL curve predicts twelve of twenty tasks are autonomy-led by 2035; this reduces per-task supervision demand from 1.0 to 0.05 person-hours per robot-hour; therefore demand drops to 4.25 person-hours per shift; therefore 4 crew supervising 3 humanoids achieves 2× headroom. Each step depends on the preceding one. The chain has one empirical anchor (NIP-10 at step 1) and four derived steps resting on §A1. If §A1 misses its 2035 gate — assessed as "High" risk in the margins register — the ratio does not advance from NIP-10's 5:1 baseline. At 5:1, a fleet of 3 humanoids requires fifteen crew, which is operationally and economically infeasible for a four-person base. The study acknowledges this sensitivity in §A18: "if the autonomy TRL curve misses the 2035 gate, the ratio reverts toward 1:1 until TRL advances." But the consequence of that reversion — the far-side base cannot operate as designed — is not developed. The attack's strongest form: the study's economics depend on the 1:2–3 ratio, the ratio depends on §A1, and §A1 is assessed as "High" risk. The study has not shown what happens to the program if the high-risk assumption fails.

**Section's current response:** Section 02-04 Section 3 presents the five-step derivation explicitly and honestly. The NIP-10 heritage is correctly characterized as the empirical baseline. The step-2 advancement claim ("factor-of-20 reduction in per-task supervision demand directly translates to a factor-of-20 improvement in supervisor ratio for those tasks") is stated as a derivation, not just an assertion. §A18 explicitly flags the risk: "if actual supervision demand per humanoid is higher (e.g., Mir-baseline maintenance absorption exceeds 40%), headroom compresses faster." The section also establishes the 2× headroom margin as an operational cushion. The §A1 conditional dependency is acknowledged in §A18: "if autonomy TRL curve misses 2035 gate, ratio reverts toward 1:1 until TRL advances."

**Assessment:** The derivation is transparent and the conditional dependency is acknowledged. The gap is that the study does not work through the consequence of assumption failure. The "reverts toward 1:1 until TRL advances" acknowledgment in §A18 is a one-sentence flag on a program-breaking consequence. If the ratio is 1:1 at IOC — one supervisor per humanoid — then the 4-crew, 3-humanoid IOC configuration has one crew member per robot plus one crew member for base operations, which is near the minimum for human safety in an isolated environment. The fleet cannot expand beyond 3 humanoids without adding crew, which defeats the economic case for the architecture. The study would need to either (a) show that the 2029 gate is a credible gate that would catch this failure early enough to redesign, or (b) show that an intermediate ratio (say 1:1.5 at IOC, reaching 1:2–3 by 2037) still produces a viable program. Neither analysis exists. The derivation chain is honest; the consequence of failure is not developed.

The secondary gap is the assumption in Step 3 that the 0.05 person-hours/robot-hour on-demand monitoring rate is defensible. The only input to this number is the paragraph in Section 02-04: "for nominally operating autonomy-led tasks, crew attention is effectively zero except for telemetry glancing (estimated 3–5 minutes per hour per humanoid for a monitoring crew member)." This is an invented number. The METERON and Lunokhod heritage data characterize direct teleoperation and supervisory control; neither provides data on the cognitive demand of on-demand monitoring of autonomy-led tasks. The 0.05 person-hours/robot-hour is parametrically derived from a first-principles estimate with no heritage anchor. If the actual monitoring load is 0.15 person-hours/robot-hour (three times higher, still low in absolute terms), the demand-side calculation rises from 4.25 to roughly 5.9 person-hours per shift, reducing headroom from 2× to 1.4×. The 2× headroom claim is sensitive to this number.

**Required action:** Section 02-04 Section 3 must add a failure-mode analysis: what does the program look like if §A1 misses the 2035 gate by two years? Specifically: (a) what ratio is achievable at IOC without the TRL 7 deliberative layer — likely 1:1 to 1:1.5 based on the fraction of tasks that can be autonomy-led at TRL 5–6; (b) what crew size is required at that ratio to supervise 3 humanoids plus maintain base operations; (c) does the program remain economically viable at that crew size; (d) at what point in the TRL curve does the ratio cross the threshold that makes the 4-crew configuration viable. This does not require redesigning the architecture — it requires showing that the 2029 gate is an effective early-warning mechanism. The 0.05 person-hours/robot-hour monitoring rate should also be flagged explicitly as a parametric estimate without heritage anchor, consistent with the study's discipline elsewhere, and a sensitivity analysis showing the headroom at 0.1 and 0.15 rates should be added to Section 02-04 Section 2.

### DA-8 — Major — "The human value floor (7 categories) is convenient post-hoc rationalization"

**Attack:** The seven-category human value floor was defined in Section 02-03 after the architecture was committed to forward deployment. A skeptical reader examining the sequence of decisions notes: (1) form factor tradespace committed to bipedal humanoid; (2) forward-deployed humans required by physics argument; (3) human value floor defined as "things the program wants humans doing." The floor is then presented as the justification for forward deployment — but forward deployment was already committed before the floor was defined. This is circular reasoning: forward deployment requires a human value floor; the human value floor was defined to require forward deployment. The attack is sharpened by noting that three of the seven categories are genuinely time-limited: Category 1 (first execution authorization) disappears as the task library matures; Category 6 (science priority decisions) is a policy choice, not a necessity; Category 7 (OOD situations flagged by the robot) depends on how well-calibrated the robot's self-model becomes. For Categories 1, 6, and 7, foundation models will plausibly provide adequate coverage by 2035 for some tasks, and by 2040 for most. If the floor erodes from seven categories to four (the genuinely non-delegatable crew-contact and life-safety categories), the forward-deployment justification narrows significantly — and the symbolic/operational continuity pillar (Section 02-04, Pillar 3) becomes the dominant driver, which is explicitly a "program argument, not a technical performance argument." A program review board that notices this shift will ask whether the technical case for forward deployment holds on its own without the policy argument.

**Section's current response:** Section 02-03 states that the human value floor "derives directly from the §A9 constraint: foundation models are not primary task executors for safety-critical operations in unstructured environments. Exploration is unstructured by definition. The floor is therefore not a temporary gap; it is a program position." Section 02-04 introduces Pillar 3 (symbolic and operational continuity) as a program argument and explicitly labels it as such: "This pillar is a program argument, not a technical performance argument. It is listed separately from the latency and situational awareness pillars because its force does not depend on the technical analysis and would survive revision of the latency numbers." The §A9 constraint is cited as the structural anchor for the floor.

**Assessment:** The study is admirably honest about Pillar 3 being a program argument. The problem is in the §A9 anchor for the floor. §A9 states: "foundation models are not primary task executors for safety-critical operations in unstructured environments." This is a 2026-calibrated constraint, explicitly marked as conservative and subject to revision at 2029 and 2035 gates. If §A9 is revised at the 2035 gate — which the study explicitly contemplates — the floor's §A9 derivation weakens. The study cannot simultaneously say "the floor is a permanent program position" and "§A9 should be revisited at each gate." The floor is either permanent (in which case it should not rest on a revisable constraint) or it is a gate-dependent assessment (in which case it should not be called permanent). This is not a semantic distinction — it is the attack vector a reviewer will use.

The specific categories most exposed are 6 (science priority) and 7 (OOD flag response). For Category 6, the study's claim is that "scientific judgment is not delegated to autonomous systems on this program regardless of model capability." This is stated as a permanent policy position — but at a multi-decade program review, reviewers will ask whether this is a value choice or an operational necessity. If a foundation model trained on the global geological survey can make better science priority decisions than a fatigued crew member, denying it that authority is not a safety decision — it is a political one about crew authority. The study should name this explicitly rather than presenting Category 6 as technically equivalent to Categories 2 and 3 (crew contact and pressurized interfaces), which are genuinely safety-critical. For Category 7, the OOD-flag response requirement depends entirely on the robot correctly identifying its OOD condition — which the study correctly notes is "the model's most poorly-calibrated capability." But if model uncertainty quantification advances to TRL 6 or higher by 2035, the OOD detection reliability improves and the human role shifts from "gating all OOD responses" to "reviewing OOD flags above a confidence threshold." This is a meaningful reduction in crew burden that the study does not acknowledge.

**Required action:** The study must make a cleaner distinction between categories that are permanent floors (crew contact, pressurized interfaces, habitat breach response, structural modification — Pillar 1 physics-grounded) and categories that are 2035-era program positions subject to gate revision (first execution authorization, science priority decisions, OOD-response gating). The current seven-category floor with uniform permanence claims is the section's most significant vulnerability to an adversarial reviewer. A two-tier floor structure — permanent/physics-grounded versus IOC-current/gate-revisable — addresses the post-hoc rationalization attack directly by showing the study understands which categories will not change and which might. Specifically: Section 02-03 Section 3 should add a paragraph after the seven-category list that sorts the categories into these two tiers and explains what evidence or TRL gate would allow revision of the gate-revisable tier. §A9's "should be revisited at each gate" language must also be reconciled with the floor's claimed permanence — either the floor is conditionally permanent (subject to §A9 gate review) or §A9 is fully permanent. Both are defensible; the current text implies both simultaneously.

## Overall Assessment

Question (b) is substantively strong but has a structural vulnerability that an adversarial reviewer will find and exploit. The vulnerability is not any single argument — each of the four attacks can be largely addressed with targeted revisions. The structural problem is that three of the four attacks converge on the same underlying weakness: the study conflates 2026-era limitations with permanent program positions, and does not adequately develop the consequence of assumption failure.

The strongest elements of the study's Question (b) case are:

1. The physics argument for forward deployment (§02-02) is genuinely airtight. The 2.78 s RTLT floor is not negotiable, and the distinction between supervision and deferred instruction at that latency is correctly drawn and concretely illustrated. No attacker can make this go away.

2. The consequence-asymmetry argument for crew-contact and life-safety gates (§02-03 Section 5, Pillar 1 of §02-04) is valid and its premises are defensible against 2035-era autonomy forecasts. A crew member with a suit breach cannot wait for a 5.56 s round-trip.

3. The cognitive load arithmetic (§02-04 Section 2) is careful, explicitly derives its components, and acknowledges its sensitivity to maintenance demand variation. It is more honest than most crew workload analyses at concept-paper stage.

The structural vulnerability: two of the four attacks (DA-5 and DA-8) target the human value floor. The floor was defined after the forward-deployment commitment, and three of its seven categories are not defensible as permanent under credible 2035-era autonomy scenarios. An adversarial reviewer who identifies these three categories as 2026-era capability assessments dressed as permanent program positions can use them to argue the floor is rationalization — and from there, argue the forward deployment commitment is political rather than technical. This is the one attack that could derail the program if it lands at a major review.

The required fix is not to abandon the floor or weaken the forward-deployment commitment. The fix is to sharpen the floor by explicitly labeling which categories are physics-grounded permanent commitments and which are IOC-era program positions subject to gate revision. This actually strengthens the study: a floor that acknowledges its temporal limits is more credible than one that claims uniform permanence for categories that will plainly evolve.

DA-6 (task-redesign path) is the second priority. The study does not demonstrate that the six jointly-executed tasks cannot be redesigned to eliminate real-time supervision requirements. This is the only attack for which the study offers no direct response. Adding a specific redesign-assessment for each jointly-executed task — showing why redesign fails or what scientific value it forfeits — closes this gap and eliminates the cleanest route to "full autonomy is cheaper."

DA-7 (supervisor ratio assumption chain) requires adding a failure-mode analysis showing the program is viable if §A1 slips two years. This is a standard risk management analysis that should exist regardless of the DA review; its absence is a gap the sections should close.

**Verdict: Defensible but not yet bulletproof. The sections address three of four attacks adequately. DA-5 and DA-8 (converging attacks on the human value floor) require substantive revision before this material faces an adversarial program review board.**
